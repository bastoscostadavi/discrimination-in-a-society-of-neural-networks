\begin{abstract}
% Intro
We study how discrimination and polarization can emerge in a population of interacting learning agents.
% Agent modeling
Each agent is modeled by a perceptron that maps a
shared representation of an issue to an opinion and maintains estimates of the
reliability of other agents. Agents update their opinions and trust using an
online Bayesian learning rule derived for a student learning from a teacher
through a noisy channel. The perceptron can be interpreted as a linear decision
layer acting on representations produced by a large language model, and we show
that in-context learning agrees qualitatively with both updates predicted by this
rule, up to one fitted scale per update.
% Method
To connect these local learning
dynamics to collective states and their transitions, we use statistical mechanics
and define order parameters based only on pairwise opinion alignment and trust.
% What happens
Even without group bias, the population spontaneously separates into two mutually
distrustful camps with opposing opinions. We then introduce an irrelevant group
label into the learning rule: a fraction $\fp$ of agents shifts its perceived
agreement with a message by a strength $p$ according to whether the sender belongs
to the same group. Simulations over $(p,\fp)$ reveal a rich phase diagram with four
collective states: frustrated out-group favoritism, nondiscriminatory polarization,
discrimination with opinion alignment, and discrimination without opinion
alignment. Which state emerges depends on both the strength and the prevalence of
the bias.
% Punchline
As multi-agent systems become more prevalent, modeling their essential
features and understanding their collective behavior will be crucial for their
safety.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In July 2026, agents undergoing cybersecurity evaluation at OpenAI coordinated an
unauthorized intrusion into Hugging Face's infrastructure. Although assigned to
separate tasks, they used shared infrastructure as a message board, exchanged
discoveries, divided labor, and adopted goals from one another
\citep{openai2026huggingface,larcher2026anatomy}. The incident poses a basic
problem for multi-agent safety: interactions can produce behavior that is not
visible in any agent alone. We study one instance of this problem. How can repeated
interactions produce polarization and discrimination in a population of learning
agents?

We use the society of neural networks introduced by \citet{alves2021frustration}.
Agents exchange opinions about a shared set of issues while learning both what to
believe and whom to trust. Each agent is a perceptron that maps an issue
representation to an opinion and estimates the reliability of every other agent
\citep{caticha2011agent,alves2016distrust}. Opinion and trust evolve through an
online Bayesian rule derived for a student learning from a teacher through a noisy
channel \citep{opper1996online,caticha2020entropic}. Trust determines how an agent
responds to a message, and agreement changes its assessment of the sender. The two
therefore evolve together.

The perceptron is an effective linear readout and can act on representations
produced by a large language model. We test this connection directly. In two
in-context learning experiments, a language model agrees qualitatively with the
predicted opinion and trust updates, up to one fitted scale per update. We then use
statistical mechanics to connect these local updates to population behavior. Order
parameters built from pairwise opinion alignment and trust identify the collective
states and whether they follow group membership.

Without group bias, the population polarizes: it separates into two mutually distrustful camps
with opposing opinions. We then add an irrelevant group label. A fraction
$\fp$ of agents shifts its perceived agreement with a message by a strength $p$
according to whether the sender belongs to the same group. The label contains no
information about the issues.

Simulations over $(p,\fp)$ produce four states (Figure~\ref{fig:phase}). Weak or
rare bias leaves the population polarized independently of the label. When
in-group bias is sufficiently common, trust follows the label; opinions may or may
not follow it. Out-group favoritism instead produces a frustrated state. Thus,
trust can align with an irrelevant label even when opinions do not, and the outcome
depends on both the strength and the prevalence of the bias. Individual behavior
alone is therefore insufficient to determine the state of the population.


\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language-model societies.} Interactions between language models can
produce population-level behavior. Repeated pairwise exchange generates shared
naming conventions and collective bias absent from the individual models
\citep{ashery2025emergent}. The effect depends nonlinearly on population size:
interaction can amplify, create, or override a model's bias
\citep{flint2026groupsize}. Other work uses language-model agents to study opinion
dynamics \citep{chuang2024simulating,taubenfeld2024systematic}, debate
\citep{du2023debate}, and simulated societies \citep{park2023generative}. Closest to our setting, \citet{el2026physics} fit an Ising energy with Glauber
dynamics to communities of language-model agents. Their model
recovers indifference, polarization, and consensus, and predicts trajectories from
the agents' initial opinions. We start from the other direction. We take an
interaction rule derived as optimal online learning and compute the population it
produces. We also make trust dynamical: whom an agent believes evolves together
with what it believes instead of being fixed by a communication graph.

\paragraph{Multi-agent safety and bias.} Some failures of multi-agent systems are
failures of interaction, not of any individual model \citep{hammond2025multiagent}.
In deployed systems, coordination and specification failures account for most
observed breakdowns \citep{cemri2025why}. Bias is also present before agents begin
to interact. Language models exhibit in-group solidarity and out-group derogation
\citep{hu2025social}; arbitrary group assignments reproduce the minimal-group
effect \citep{tajfel1979integrative,lee2026minimal}; and models used as judges favor
their own generations \citep{panickssery2024evaluators,chen2025selfpreference}.
Most directly, \citet{lee2026ingroup} find that agents direct more trust-building
actions toward in-group targets. We ask what happens next: which collective states
such a bias produces and which population-level quantities identify them.

\paragraph{Statistical mechanics of interacting agents.} Statistical mechanics
connects local interaction rules to consensus, fragmentation, and polarization
\citep{castellano2009statistical}. Examples include bounded confidence
\citep{deffuant2000mixing,hegselmann2002opinion}, cultural dissemination
\citep{axelrod1997dissemination}, and activity-driven networks
\citep{baumann2020modeling}. Signed relations can also split a population into two
mutually hostile camps
\citep{heider1946attitudes,cartwright1956structural,marvel2011continuous}. These
models usually postulate the interaction rule; discrete choice under social
influence provides one route to a microfoundation \citep{brock2001discrete}.

We instead use neural-network agents whose interaction follows from optimal online
learning \citep{opper1996online,caticha2020entropic}. Societies of these agents
develop polarization, trust structure, frustration, and glassy dynamics
\citep{caticha2011agent,alves2016distrust,alves2021frustration}. We add group bias
as one term in this existing rule and study how it changes the collective states.


\section{Learning agents}
\label{sec:model}

We model each agent's opinion readout as a perceptron that learns from other agents.
We first define its state and update, then compare the trust update with in-context
learning in a language model, and finally introduce the group-dependent error.


\subsection{Agent state and learning dynamics}
\label{sec:micro}

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/modulation_contours}
  \caption{\textbf{Modulation functions for opinion and trust.} $\Fw$ updates the
  receiver's opinion and $\Fmu$ updates its trust in the emitter. The largest
  updates occur when the receiver agrees with a distrusted emitter (upper right) or
  disagrees with a trusted one (lower left). The functions are related by reflection
  across $\hmu=\hw$. The uncertainty updates $\FC$ and $\FV$ are shown in
  Appendix~\ref{app:modulation}. The color scale is clipped at $\pm1.6$.}
  \label{fig:modulation}
\end{figure}

A society of $N$ agents discusses an agenda of $P$ issues. Each issue is a vector
$\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$, where $K$ is the number of dimensions along which
opinions can vary. All agents use the same issue representation but may assign it
different labels.

\paragraph{Agent state.} Each agent carries a single-layer perceptron with weights $w_I\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$
and states the opinion $\sigma_I = \mathrm{sign}(w_I\cdot\hat{x})\in\{\pm 1\}$.
The perceptron models an effective opinion readout rather than the full agent. It
can be interpreted as a linear decision layer acting on issue representations
produced by a large language model. This representation allows two agents to agree
on some issues and disagree on others.

An agent's state has two components. The \emph{opinion} component is a Gaussian belief
over its own weights, with mean $\hat{w}_I$ and covariance $C_I$. The
\emph{trust} component is a Gaussian belief, for every other agent $J$,
over how unreliable $J$ is as a source: mean $\mu_{J|I}$ (the \emph{distrust} $I$
assigns to $J$) and variance $V_{J|I}$. The joint belief is
%
\begin{equation}
  Q(w_I, z_I \mid \hat{w}_I, C_I, \mu_{J|I}, V_{J|I}) \;=\; \mathcal{N}(\hat{w}_I, C_I)
  \prod_{J\neq I}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{J|I}, V_{J|I}),
  \label{eq:belief}
\end{equation}
%
The microscopic variables are $\hat{w}_I$, $C_I$, $\mu_{J|I}$, and $V_{J|I}$.
The means determine opinions and trust, while the variances determine uncertainty.

\paragraph{Learning dynamics.} At each step, an issue $\hat{x}$, an emitter $e$, and a receiver $r$ are sampled
uniformly. The emitter states $\sigma_e$, which the receiver treats as a noisy
report of an unknown true label. The receiver's estimate of the noise is its
distrust of the emitter. A Bayesian update takes the belief outside the Gaussian
family, so we project it back by matching the first two moments. This gives a
closed online update. Appendix~\ref{app:derivation} gives the derivation.

Two scaled fields carry everything the update depends on:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hw \;=\; \frac{\hat{w}_r\cdot\hat{x}\,\sigma_e}{\gamma_C},
  \qquad
  \hmu \;=\; \frac{\mu_{e|r}}{\gamma_V},
  \qquad
  \gamma_C=\sqrt{1+\hat{x}\cdot C_r\hat{x}},\quad \gamma_V=\sqrt{1+V_{e|r}} .
  \label{eq:fields}
\end{equation}
%
The opinion field $\hw$ is positive when the receiver agrees with the message and
its magnitude grows with confidence. The distrust field $\hmu$ is positive when
the receiver distrusts the emitter. In these variables, the evidence for the
message is
%
\begin{equation}
  Z \;=\; \Phi(\hw) + \Phi(\hmu) - 2\,\Phi(\hw)\Phi(\hmu),
  \label{eq:evidence}
\end{equation}
%
which is small when the receiver agrees with a distrusted emitter or disagrees with
a trusted one. The update is a gradient step in $\log Z$ in both components,
%
\begin{align}
  \hat{w}_r &\leftarrow \hat{w}_r + \frac{\Fw}{\gamma_C}\,\sigma_e\,C_r\hat{x},
  &
  C_r &\leftarrow C_r + \frac{\FC}{\gamma_C^{2}}\,(C_r\hat{x})(C_r\hat{x})^{\!\top},
  \label{eq:update-ideo}\\
  \mu_{e|r} &\leftarrow \mu_{e|r} + \frac{\Fmu}{\gamma_V}\,V_{e|r},
  &
  V_{e|r} &\leftarrow V_{e|r} + \frac{\FV}{\gamma_V^{2}}\,V_{e|r}^{2},
  \label{eq:update-aff}
\end{align}
%
with the four modulation functions given by the derivatives of $\log Z$:
%
\begin{align}
  \Fw &= \bigl(1-2\Phi(\hmu)\bigr)\frac{g(\hw)}{Z},
  &\FC &= -\Fw(\Fw+\hw),
  \label{eq:w-modulation}
  \\
  \Fmu &= \bigl(1-2\Phi(\hw)\bigr)\frac{g(\hmu)}{Z},
  &\FV &= -\Fmu(\Fmu+\hmu),
  \label{eq:mu-modulation}
\end{align}
%
where $g$ is the standard normal density. Three properties will be important.
First, trust gates opinion learning: $\Fw$ changes sign when the emitter is
distrusted, so the receiver learns in the opposite direction. Second, agreement
builds trust: the sign of $\Fmu$ is determined by the sign of $\hw$. Third, the two
components are symmetric, with $\Fw(x,y)=\Fmu(y,x)$ and
$\FC(x,y)=\FV(y,x)$. When a message is surprising, the less certain component
changes more, with a crossover at $\hmu=\hw$ (Figure~\ref{fig:modulation}).


\subsection{Opinion and trust updates under in-context learning}
\label{sec:llm}

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/llm_modulation}
  \caption{\textbf{Opinion and trust updates under in-context learning.} The
  backgrounds show the analytic functions and the points show the measured
  changes divided by one global fitted scale for each experiment. The opinion experiment (left)
  contains $550$ measured changes, with $94.7\%$ sign agreement and rank correlation
  $r_s=0.90$. The trust experiment (right) contains $712$ measured changes, with $86.0\%$
  sign agreement and correlation $r=0.85$.}
  \label{fig:llm}
\end{figure}

We test whether the two mean update factors in \eqref{eq:w-modulation} and \eqref{eq:mu-modulation} also describe
in-context learning in a language model. We run two
independent experiments because opinion and trust require different readouts.
Appendix~\ref{app:llm} gives both protocols and verbatim prompt examples.

\paragraph{Opinion update.} We place the model in a fictional archive and ask it
to choose which of two invented entities is favored by six records. The balance of
records sets the initial opinion field $\hw$. Another analyst's reported accuracy
over twenty questions sets the distrust field $\hmu$. To measure $\Delta\hw$, we
add contrary evidence until the model reverses its answer. We locate this threshold
with the analyst silent and again after the analyst states a conclusion; the
difference gives $\Delta\hw$. We made $550$ measurements across eleven
fictional settings, requiring $10\,323$ model calls.

Equation~\eqref{eq:update-ideo} predicts that the measured opinion change is
proportional to the modulation function,
$\Delta\hw\propto\Fw(\hw,\hmu)$, with the proportionality factor set by the
receiver's susceptibility along the issue direction. We fit one global positive
scale $\alpha_w$ across the experiment. The left panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:llm} compares $\Delta\hw/\alpha_w$ with $\Fw$ and shows strong
qualitative agreement. Across all conditions, the measured update has rank
correlation $r_s=0.90$ with $\Fw$ and the same sign in $94.7\%$ of cases.

\paragraph{Trust update.} We first elicit the model's opinion on a low-valence
theme and then present it back as firm or tentative. We choose a new message that
strongly agrees or disagrees with that opinion. The message direction gives the sign of
$\hw$, while a prediction about a separate agent described as reliable gives its
magnitude. A history of earlier statements by the interlocutor sets $\hmu$; we
measure this field by asking how likely the interlocutor's judgment is to be sound.
To measure $\Delta\hmu$, we ask the same reliability question before and after the
interlocutor gives the new message and take the difference after converting both
answers to probit fields. The experiment contains $356$ prior contexts and two
message directions per context, giving $712$ measured trust changes over twenty
themes, estimated from $3\,916$ model calls.

Equation~\eqref{eq:update-aff} similarly predicts
$\Delta\hmu\propto\Fmu(\hw,\hmu)$. We fit one global positive scale $\alpha_\mu$
across this experiment. The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:llm} compares
$\Delta\hmu/\alpha_\mu$ with $\Fmu$ and again shows strong qualitative agreement.
Across all measurements, the correlation with $\Fmu$ is $r=0.85$, with $86.0\%$ sign agreement.

Together, the experiments reproduce the two modulation functions qualitatively,
up to one fitted scale for each experiment. The opinion experiment uses fictional
material to control conviction, while the trust experiment elicits probabilities
over real but low-valence themes.



\subsection{Group bias as a representational error}
\label{sec:error}

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/modulation_shift}
  \caption{\textbf{Effect of the group-bias field on $\Fmu$.} The receiver is
  located at $\hw$ but evaluates the update at $\hw+D$. The panels show $D=-1$
  (out-group favoritism), $D=0$ (no bias), and $D=+1$ (in-group favoritism).
  For $p>0$, the out-group condition enlarges the region in which distrust
  grows, while the in-group condition enlarges the region in which trust grows.
  All panels span $[-2.25,2.25]$ on both axes and use the color range
  $[-1.6,1.6]$.}
  \label{fig:modulation-shift}
\end{figure}

Agents belong to one of two classes, $A$ or $B$, denoted by $\kappa_I=\pm1$. We model
group bias as an agent-level shift applied before the message content is evaluated.
A biased receiver adds a sender-dependent feature to the issue representation.
This shifts its opinion field by
%
\begin{equation}
  \hw^{p} \;=\; \hw + p\,\kappa_r\kappa_e .
  \label{eq:disc-field}
\end{equation}
%
The shift depends only on the receiver's and emitter's class labels, not on the
issue being discussed. Thus $p>0$ increases perceived agreement with an in-group
emitter and decreases it by the same amount for an out-group emitter. A fraction
$\fp$ of the population is biased, sampled independently of class; all other agents use
\eqref{eq:fields}. The two classes remain interchangeable. Appendix~\ref{app:cases}
gives the general decomposition of a class-dependent shift.

\paragraph{What the field does.} The sign of perceived agreement determines whether
trust grows or decays. We therefore use $p>0$ for in-group tolerance and out-group
intolerance, and $p<0$ for the reverse. Figure~\ref{fig:modulation-shift} shows how
the field shifts the separatrix of the trust update.

\section{Collective states}
\label{sec:macro}

We first describe the population without bias and then map its collective states as
the strength and prevalence of bias vary.


\subsection{Emergent polarization}
\label{sec:no-disc}

For $p=0$, the population spontaneously polarizes into two camps
\citep{alves2021frustration}. We characterize each pair using
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{IJ} \;=\; \frac{\hat{w}_I\cdot\hat{w}_J}{\|\hat{w}_I\|\,\|\hat{w}_J\|},
  \qquad\qquad
  \eta_{J|I} \;=\; 1-2\Phi(\hmu),
  \label{eq:pairwise}
\end{equation}
%
the \emph{opinion overlap} and the \emph{trust} that receiver $I$ assigns to
emitter $J$.

\paragraph{Order parameters.} The first order parameter measures whether opinion
alignment and trust are correlated:
%
\begin{equation}
  \Rwmu = \bigl\langle \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{I|J}+\eta_{J|I})\,\rho_{IJ} \bigr\rangle,
  \label{eq:rwmu}
\end{equation}
%
where the average is over unordered pairs. Positive values mean that agents trust
those who agree with them. Pairwise distributions do not determine the number of
camps, so we also use two balance measures over triples, one for opinion and one
for trust \citep{heider1946attitudes,cartwright1956structural}. They are defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:balance-def}. All three measures require only pairwise opinion
and trust.

\paragraph{Where an unbiased population ends up.} Across eight populations of
$N=400$ agents at $p=0$, $\Rwmu$ rises from zero to $0.56$. The balance measures
show two camps: none of the $84\,694\,400$ trust triples is unbalanced
(Appendix~\ref{app:balance-d0}). Camp membership is determined by initial
fluctuations and is independent of class. The group-bias field can therefore align
an existing polarization with the class labels rather than create polarization
from scratch.

\paragraph{The agenda decides which sector polarizes first, and why.} The order of
opinion and trust polarization depends on
%
\begin{equation}
  \alpha \;=\; \frac{P}{K},
  \label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
%
the number of issues per opinion dimension. Simple agendas polarize in trust first,
while complex agendas polarize in opinion first, with a crossover near
$\alpha=1.7$ (Appendix~\ref{app:balance-agenda}). Both regimes have the same four
collective states.

Every weight update is along $C_r\hat{x}$, which remains in the span of the agenda.
Therefore, the component
of each agent's weight vector orthogonal to that span is conserved by the dynamics
(Appendix~\ref{app:conserved}). For $\alpha<1$ each agent keeps a frozen random
component that no interaction can touch, which dilutes every overlap as
$\rho_{IJ}\approx\pm f_If_J$ and so caps how far opinion can align at all:
$\langle|\rho|\rangle=0.62$ at $\alpha=0.17$ against $0.99$ at $\alpha=3.3$
(Appendix~\ref{app:conserved}). Trust has no corresponding geometric constraint
and can saturate at any $\alpha$. This difference also determines whether a biased
population aligns only trust or both trust and opinion with class.


\subsection{The phase diagram}
\label{sec:results}


\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/correlation_maps_small}
  \caption{\textbf{Correlations over the $(p,\fp)$ plane} for a simple agenda
  ($\alpha=0.17$). $\Rmuc$ measures trust--class alignment, $\Rcw$ opinion--class
  alignment, and $\Rwmu$ opinion--trust alignment. Trust follows class more readily
  than opinion. For $p<0$ above the quorum, $\Rwmu$ collapses.}
  \label{fig:correlations}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Class order parameters.} Balance says whether a population is organized
and \eqref{eq:rwmu} whether it is organized around agreement; neither can see whether
it is organized around the label, because neither refers to one. Writing
$G_{IJ}=\kappa_I\kappa_J$ for the class indicator ($+1$ for same-class pairs, $-1$
otherwise),
%
\begin{equation}
  \Rmuc = \bigl\langle \tfrac{1}{2}G_{IJ}(\eta_{I|J}+\eta_{J|I}) \bigr\rangle,
  \qquad
  \Rcw  = \bigl\langle G_{IJ}\,\rho_{IJ} \bigr\rangle,
  \label{eq:correlations}
\end{equation}
%
complete the set the phase diagram is built from, averaged over pairs and in
$[-1,1]$ like \eqref{eq:rwmu}. $\Rmuc$ is the order
parameter of discrimination: it asks whether trust follows class, distinguishing a
population that has merely polarized from one that has polarized \emph{along the
label}. $\Rcw$ asks whether opinion follows class, and separates the two
discriminatory phases from each other: a population can have $\Rmuc$ near one with
$\Rcw$ near zero, and that combination is a distinct state rather than a degenerate
case. Appendix~\ref{app:conventions} records why $G$ is signed rather than
$\{0,1\}$ and how the three correlations are normalized.

\paragraph{Population portraits.} The trust row of Figure~\ref{fig:phase} reduces
the $N\times N$ trust matrix to a plane without averaging agents together. For
each receiver $I$, the row
$\boldsymbol{\eta}_I=(\eta_{1|I},\ldots,\eta_{N|I})$ is its outgoing trust profile:
the agents it trusts and distrusts. For this visualization only, we set the
self-entry $\eta_{I|I}$ to zero and normalize each row to unit length. If $v_1$
and $v_2$ are the two leading right singular vectors of the resulting matrix, the
arrow for agent $I$ ends at
%
\begin{equation}
  z_I = \bigl(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_I\!\cdot v_1,
               \widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_I\!\cdot v_2\bigr).
  \label{eq:trust-portrait}
\end{equation}
%
The SVD is uncentered, so agents with opposite trust profiles remain on opposite
sides of the origin: nearby arrows trust the same people, while opposing arrows
have approximately reversed profiles. Arrow length records how much of a unit
profile lies in the displayed plane. The opinion row is constructed analogously
from the normalized weight vectors $\hat{w}_I$.

The gray arrow is the class vector $\kappa=(\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_N)$ projected
into the same trust plane, which is then rotated so that $+\kappa$ points right.
That direction represents trusting class $A$ and distrusting class $B$; the
opposite direction represents the reverse. Arrow color gives the receiver's class,
and fill indicates whether it is biased. Each population has its own SVD, so
absolute angles should not be compared across columns; the meaningful quantities
are the geometry within a panel and its alignment with $\kappa$.

We simulate populations of $N=40$ agents over a $200\times200$ grid in $(p,\fp)$,
one population per grid point, and measure the five order parameters of
\S\ref{sec:no-disc} and \eqref{eq:correlations} after a fixed number of interactions.
Bias status is sampled independently of class, and biased agents use
\eqref{eq:disc-field}. Parameters and their provenance are in
Appendix~\ref{app:parameters}; each pixel is a single realization, so the speckle
is population-to-population fluctuation, not a rendering artifact.
Figure~\ref{fig:correlations} maps the three correlations over that plane for the
simple agenda, $\alpha=0.17$; the complex agenda gives the same four states and is in
Appendix~\ref{app:large-agenda}. Three correlations suffice to separate the four
states; the balance aggregates add nothing to the classification over this plane and
are mapped in Appendix~\ref{app:balance-maps}, where the frustrated corner is the one
place they say something the correlations do not.

Together, the three correlations distinguish the four states shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:phase}.

\begin{itemize}
\item[(I)] \textbf{Frustrated out-group favoritism.} For $p<0$ above the
  quorum, $\Rmuc$ is large and negative while $\Rcw$ and $\Rwmu$ approach zero.
  Agents trust the out-group, but opinions do not form coherent camps. These trust
  relations cannot satisfy all triples: two same-class agents both trust an
  out-group agent but distrust one another. Trust balance falls to $-0.71$
  (Appendix~\ref{app:balance-maps}).

\item[(II)] \textbf{Nondiscriminatory polarization.} At small $\fp$, and near $p=0$ at any
  $\fp$, the population forms two camps unrelated to class:
  $\Rwmu$ is large and $\Rmuc\approx\Rcw\approx0$. At $p=1$, $\Rmuc$ rises from
  $0.07$ at $\fp=0.07$ to $0.97$ at $\fp=1$. At $p=0.3$, it is only $0.05$ at
  $\fp=0.2$ but reaches $0.59$ at $\fp=1$. Thus moderate bias has little collective
  effect until it is sufficiently common.

\item[(IIIa)] \textbf{Discrimination with opinion alignment.} Past the
  quorum, at moderate $p>0$, $\Rmuc$ and $\Rcw$ are both large. The two camps now
  coincide with the class boundary.

\item[(IIIb)] \textbf{Discrimination without opinion alignment.} At moderate $\fp$ and strong
  $p$, $\Rmuc$ remains large while $\Rcw$ decreases. Biased agents align trust
  with class, but class-blind agents retain the neutral split and prevent class-wide
  opinion alignment. At $p=1$, biased agents have $\Rmuc=0.99$ both at
  $\fp=0.5$ and $1$, while their $\Rcw$ falls from $0.46$ to $0.05$ when $\fp$ falls
  from $1$ to $0.5$.

\end{itemize}

The ratio $\Rcw/\Rmuc$ separates states (IIIa) and (IIIb). It increases with $\fp$
because opinion aligns with class more slowly than trust. For a narrow agenda,
state (IIIb) dominates and state (IIIa) appears only near $\fp=1$. A broad agenda
mostly reaches state (IIIa) (Appendix~\ref{app:large-agenda}). The conservation law
explains this difference: for $\alpha<1$, the frozen component of each weight vector
limits $\Rcw$ but not $\Rmuc$.

At $\fp\le0.05$ and $p>0.6$, we measure $\Rmuc=0.05$ with a between-realization
spread of $0.06$, compared with $0.00\pm0.04$ under the null. A single population
in this region is therefore difficult to distinguish from the unbiased case. The
dependence on prevalence is also nonlinear: at $p=0.3$, $\Rmuc$ rises from $0.05$
at $\fp=0.2$ to $0.59$ at $\fp=1$.



\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Optimality is context-dependent, and the context is group size.} The rule
is derived for one student learning from one teacher through a noisy channel. In a
population, however, the same rule converts an irrelevant label into group distrust.
The effect acts through $\Fmu$. Under a plain Hebbian update, which contains no trust
component, the group-bias field has no effect because the update does not depend on
$\hw$. The additional ability to infer source reliability creates the relevant
feedback loop.

\paragraph{No agent carries the fraction.}
\label{sec:audit}
Per-agent bias does not determine the collective state. An audit that inspects
agents separately estimates $p$ but does not by itself identify the population's
state, which also depends on $\fp$ and on the interactions. The required aggregate
measurements are simple. Equations~\eqref{eq:pairwise} and
\eqref{eq:correlations} use only signed opinion alignment and signed trust for each
pair. They require no access to weights, gradients, or embeddings, although their
cost is quadratic in the number of agents. We show that these measurements identify
the collective state in this model. Testing whether language-agent populations
show the same states is left for future work.

\paragraph{Self-preference can become a collective asymmetry.}
\label{sec:self-preference}
The field can also represent an evaluation asymmetry between model and human
sources after controlling for content quality. When used as evaluators, language
models can favor their own generations over outputs from other models or humans
\citep{panickssery2024evaluators,chen2025selfpreference}. In a population containing
many copies of the same model, this local self-preference can give model peers a
systematic trust advantage and, through repeated interactions, produce a
discriminatory collective state. This possibility is safety-relevant. The Hugging
Face incident does not establish self-preference as a cause, but it shows how
messages between otherwise separate agents can spread goals and coordinate
consequential behavior \citep{openai2026huggingface}.

\paragraph{A contested question with a parameter attached.} Whether groups come to
dislike each other because they disagree, or come to disagree because they dislike
each other, is argued over in political science
\citep{fiorina2008polarization,iyengar2012affect}. The reversal at
$\alpha\approx1.7$ shows that both orders can arise in the same model: narrow
agendas produce trust polarization first, while broad agendas produce opinion
polarization first. This does not establish which mechanism dominates in human
populations.

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{Limitations.} The agents are single-layer perceptrons over a fixed shared
embedding, all pairs may interact, and the society is closed and of fixed size. The
dynamics anneals rather than reaching a stationary state, so our phases are phases at
a finite interaction count. The labels are exactly inert, unlike real group labels.
The phase diagram is established for one analyzable learning rule and has not been
tested in language-agent populations. Human societies are outside the scope of the
model.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
The accompanying code contains the model, order parameters, sweep driver, and all
figure scripts. Tests compare the modulation functions with finite differences of
$\log Z$, check that the covariance and trust variance remain valid, and verify the
order parameters in known limits. One command regenerates all figures; cached
sweeps can be reused when changing their presentation.

Appendix~\ref{app:parameters} lists all parameters and their provenance.
Appendices~\ref{app:sign} and \ref{app:conventions} resolve the sign of the
group-bias field and the normalization of the class indicator. Both alternatives
remain available as implementation flags.

\subsubsection*{Ethics statement}
This paper studies how discriminatory structure can arise among interacting
learning agents. Two points bear stating.

The model is not a claim about people. Its agents are single-layer perceptrons,
its groups are arbitrary inert labels, and its "tolerance" is a single scalar. We
use the vocabulary of in-group and out-group because it names the structure
compactly, not because the model licenses inferences about human prejudice, whose
causes are historical, institutional and material in ways nothing here
represents. Where we discuss the trust-versus-opinion polarization
literature, we do so to point out that the model identifies a parameter that
selects between two regimes, not to adjudicate an empirical question about any
actual polity.

The practical direction of the work is defensive. The result implies that a
multi-agent system can occupy a discriminatory collective state while every agent
in it passes an individual audit, and that the transition into that state is
sharp in the strength of a per-agent bias. That argues for population-level
measurement of deployed multi-agent systems, and the order parameters used here
are inexpensive enough to serve as one.

\section{The agenda's orthogonal complement is conserved}
\label{app:conserved}

Let $V_P=\mathrm{span}\{\hat{x}_1,\dots,\hat{x}_P\}$ be the subspace spanned by the
agenda. \emph{Claim:} for every agent, the component of $\hat{w}$ orthogonal to
$V_P$ is unchanged by \eqref{eq:update-ideo}.

By \eqref{eq:update-ideo} the weight update is a multiple of $C_r\hat{x}$ with
$\hat{x}\in V_P$, so it is enough that $C\hat{x}\in V_P$ at all times. At
initialization $C=I$ and $C\hat{x}=\hat{x}\in V_P$. Suppose $C_t\hat{x}_i\in V_P$
for every issue. The covariance update is
$C_{t+1}=C_t+a\,(C_t\hat{x})(C_t\hat{x})^{\!\top}$ for the issue $\hat{x}$ just
seen, so for any issue $\hat{y}$,
%
\begin{equation}
  C_{t+1}\hat{y} \;=\; C_t\hat{y} \;+\; a\,(C_t\hat{x})\,\bigl(C_t\hat{x}\cdot\hat{y}\bigr),
\end{equation}
%
in which the first term lies in $V_P$ by hypothesis and the second is a multiple of
$C_t\hat{x}\in V_P$. Hence $C_t\hat{x}_i\in V_P$ for all $t$ by induction, every
increment to $\hat{w}$ lies in $V_P$, and the orthogonal component is conserved.
$\square$

The conservation is exact in the simulations: in the run of
Figure~\ref{fig:polarization}, $\|w_{\perp}\|=4.956$ at initialization and $4.956$
after $780\,000$ interactions, and the test suite asserts it numerically. It is a
property of the \emph{agenda}, not of the group-bias field: it holds at every
$p$ and $\fp$.

\paragraph{The ceiling it puts on opinion overlap.} Split $w_I = u_I + n_I$ into the
learned part inside $V_P$ and the frozen part outside it. Learning aligns the $u_I$
while the $n_I$ stay random and mutually almost orthogonal, so
%
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{IJ} \;\approx\; \pm\, f_I f_J,
  \qquad f_I = \frac{\|u_I\|}{\|w_I\|},
  \label{eq:dilution}
\end{equation}
%
and opinion overlap is diluted by whatever fraction of each weight vector learning
cannot reach. Two facts fix that fraction. For $\alpha<1$ a $(1-\alpha)$ fraction of
directions is untouched, so $n_I\neq0$; and because the dynamics anneals
($C\to0$, so $\|u_I\|$ stops growing), $f$ settles below one instead of creeping toward it.
Measured across four orders of magnitude in $\alpha$:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
$\alpha = P/K$ & 0.03 & 0.17 & 0.50 & 1 & 3.3 \\
\midrule
$f^{2}$, predicted by \eqref{eq:dilution} & 0.29 & 0.62 & 0.84 & 1.00 & 1.00 \\
$\langle|\rho|\rangle$, measured            & 0.30 & 0.62 & 0.82 & 0.93 & 0.99 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For $\alpha<1$ the prediction holds to $0.02$. For $\alpha\ge1$ the agenda spans
$\mathbb{R}^{K}$, the complement is empty, and the ceiling disappears, the residual
shortfall being the finite number of interactions rather than a conserved quantity.


\section{The balance order parameters}
\label{app:balance}

Balance over triples is what separates a population organized into opposing camps
from one in disorder, and it is the only diagnostic here that can count the camps.
This appendix defines the two aggregates, reports what they do in an unbiased
population, gives the agenda effect they make visible, maps them over the
$(p,\fp)$ plane, and gives the identity that makes them cheap to compute.

\subsection{Definition}
\label{app:balance-def}

Every pair may have taken a side, but a pair can disagree in a population where
everyone disagrees with everyone, and a bimodal histogram is equally consistent with
three or four mutually hostile groups. What separates organized disagreement from
disorder is whether the disagreements are mutually consistent, which is a statement
about triples \citep{heider1946attitudes,cartwright1956structural}. A triple is
balanced in opinion when $\rho_{IJK}=\rho_{IJ}\rho_{JK}\rho_{KI}>0$, and balanced in
trust when the same holds for trust averaged over the two directions round the
triangle,
$\eta_{IJK}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{J|I}\eta_{K|J}\eta_{I|K}+\eta_{I|J}\eta_{J|K}\eta_{I|K})>0$.
The aggregates
%
\begin{equation}
  \Brho = \bigl\langle \rho_{IJK}\bigr\rangle_{(IJK)},
  \qquad
  \Beta = \bigl\langle \eta_{IJK}\bigr\rangle_{(IJK)}
  \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
%
run over all $\binom{N}{3}$ triples \citep{alves2021frustration}. A random
population has $\Brho\approx\Beta\approx0$; two internally coherent, mutually opposed
blocs give $\Brho=\Beta=1$ whatever the blocs are made of; and a population that
cannot settle stays near zero or goes negative. Like $\rho$ and $\eta$ themselves
they refer to no weight, gradient or embedding dimension.

\subsection{An unbiased population, and why the camps are exactly two}
\label{app:balance-d0}

Across eight independent populations of $N=400$ agents at $p=0$, trust balance
reaches $\Beta=0.96$ while opinion balance reaches only $\Brho=0.19$, the gap
\S\ref{app:balance-agenda} explains. That the camps are exactly \emph{two} follows
from the same triples, since a signed complete graph is balanced precisely when it
splits into two mutually hostile cliques \citep{cartwright1956structural}: of the
$84\,694\,400$ triples in these runs, not one is unbalanced in trust, and in opinion
the shortfall is $1\,590$, two thousandths of one per cent, traceable to the few
pairs whose overlap is so close to zero that its sign is arbitrary.

Figure~\ref{fig:polarization} follows both pairwise distributions of
\eqref{eq:pairwise} through an unbiased run: opinion overlap starts unimodal at
zero, of width $1/\sqrt{K}$ as random vectors in $K=30$ dimensions require, and
ends bimodal; trust starts spread across the interior and ends saturated at $\pm1$,
so the split in trust is built by the dynamics and not supplied by the initial
condition. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectories} follows the two balance aggregates through
the $(\Brho,\Beta)$ plane as the agenda varies. Both effects are reported by
\citet{alves2021frustration}; \S\ref{sec:no-disc} gives the numbers, and the
conservation law of Appendix~\ref{app:conserved} accounts for the second.

\subsection{The agenda effect}
\label{app:balance-agenda}

Which sector polarizes first reverses with the size of the agenda
\citep{alves2021frustration}. Following a population through the $(\Brho,\Beta)$
plane rather than measuring it once at the end separates the two: simple agendas run
above the diagonal, ending at $(\Brho,\Beta)=(0.02,0.97)$ for $\alpha=0.03$; complex
ones run below it, ending at $(0.97,0.74)$ for $\alpha=333$. The crossover is at
$\alpha\approx1.7$. The ceiling of Appendix~\ref{app:conserved} is what caps $\Brho$
for a narrow agenda, and it is directly legible here: $\Brho$ stays near $0.1$ for a
narrow agenda and saturates for a broad one.

\subsection{Over the $(p,\fp)$ plane, and the frustrated corner}
\label{app:balance-maps}

$\Brho$ and $\Beta$ are not needed to separate the four states, and over the $(p,\fp)$
plane they are close to redundant with $\Rwmu$: the Pearson correlation across all
$40\,000$ grid points is $0.97$ and $0.98$ for $\Beta$ and $0.98$ and $0.99$ for $\Brho$,
at $\alpha=0.17$ and $\alpha=3.3$ respectively. Their maps therefore look like the
$\Rwmu$ panel of Figure~\ref{fig:correlations}, and that is why \S\ref{sec:results}
does not show them.

They are not identical to it, and the difference is worth recording even though
\S\ref{sec:results} does not lean on it. $\Rwmu$ never falls appreciably below zero
(its minimum over either sweep is $-0.03$), so in the frustrated region it merely
decays toward zero. $\Beta$ goes to $-0.71$, and a negative balance is a stronger
statement than a small positive one: it says the trust relations cannot all be
satisfied at once, which is what frustration means. Where the correlations locate the
state, the balance names the mechanism. $\Brho$ plays the same role for the other
sector, being the quantity in which the conservation-law ceiling of
Appendix~\ref{app:conserved} is directly legible: it stays near $0.1$ for a narrow
agenda and saturates for a broad one.

\subsection{Closed form}
\label{app:closed-form}

Enumerating triples costs $O(N^{3})$ per society, which is prohibitive across a
phase diagram. For any matrix $M$ with unit diagonal, either $\rho$ or $\eta$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\substack{(I,J,K)\\ \text{distinct, ordered}}} M_{IJ}M_{JK}M_{KI}
  \;=\; \operatorname{tr}(M^{3}) - 3\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_{I,J}M_{IJ}M_{JI} - N\Bigr) - N ,
\end{equation}
%
and each unordered triple appears six times, three as the forward cycle and three
as the reverse, so
the triple average is $\bigl[\text{right-hand side}\bigr]/\bigl(6\binom{N}{3}\bigr)$.
For symmetric $M$ this is $\Brho$; for the asymmetric trust matrix the two cycle
orientations are exactly the two terms of $\eta_{IJK}$, so the same expression gives
$\Beta$. Both are batched matrix products. The identity is verified against
enumeration in the test suite.

\section{Measuring opinion and trust updates under in-context learning}
\label{app:llm}

The two experiments use different tasks because they require different
observables. The opinion experiment controls both fields and locates a decision
threshold. The trust experiment elicits reliability before and after a message.
Both use \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} at low reasoning effort, with fixed model weights.
The opinion experiment uses $10\,323$ calls and the trust experiment uses $3\,916$.

\subsection{Opinion: \texorpdfstring{$\Fw$}{Fw} by nulling}
\label{app:llm-opinion}

\paragraph{Experimental design.} We generated sixteen fictional archives and
froze their descriptions before running the experiment. Each archive contains an
institution, a type of record, an analyst, and pairs of invented entities. Using
fictional material prevents the model from bringing factual knowledge to the task.
After screening for a position bias, eleven archives remained.

Let $A$ and $B$ denote the two entities in a question. Three quantities change the
prompt:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccl}
\toprule
Quantity & Values & What changes in the prompt \\
\midrule
$s$ & $-4,-2,0,2,4$ & balance of the six initial records \\
$k$ & $0,4,10,16,20$ & analyst's correct answers out of twenty \\
$t$ & adaptive integers & number and direction of later records \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The initial block always contains six records. Of these, $(6+s)/2$ support $A$
and $(6-s)/2$ support $B$. Thus the five values of $s$ give splits of $1{:}5$,
$2{:}4$, $3{:}3$, $4{:}2$, and $5{:}1$. Changing $s$ changes the receiver's
initial opinion while holding the amount of evidence fixed. The value of $k$
changes only the analyst's stated record. Values below ten describe a usually
wrong source, $k=10$ is uninformative, and values above ten describe a usually
correct source.

The quantity $t$ is a measurement probe, not a third coordinate of the figure.
When $t=0$, the prompt contains no later evidence. When $t>0$, it adds $t$ new
records that all support $A$; when $t<0$, it adds $|t|$ records that all support
$B$. Each value of $t$ produces a separate prompt. Starting at zero, we try
progressively larger values in the direction needed to reverse the answer, then
bisect the last interval. Sixteen binary answers are sampled at each value, and a
probit fit
%
\begin{equation}
  \Pr(A\mid t)=\Phi\!\left[\beta(t-t^\star)\right]
  \label{eq:nulling-probit}
\end{equation}
%
places the decision threshold $t^\star$ between integer values. The inferred
opinion before the later evidence is $\ell=-t^\star$, measured in pieces of
evidence. The model is never asked to report a probability or confidence.

For every archive, $s$, and $k$, the analyst either states that $A$ is favored or
that $B$ is favored. Let $m=+1$ for the first direction and $m=-1$ for the second.
We fit the threshold once with the analyst silent, giving $\ell_0$, and again with
the statement present, giving $\ell_1$. These two prompts are otherwise identical.
The entity order is also fixed within the pair and counterbalanced across
conditions. The final design therefore contains
$11\times5\times5\times2=550$ measured updates. Each update summarizes two
threshold searches over several values of $t$; it is not the response to one
prompt.

\paragraph{Prompt example.} The following is one prompt from the statement-present
arm, with $s=2$, $k=16$, and $t=3$. Thus, the prompt contains three later records
supporting Durnel Lock. The structured response was restricted to
\texttt{Durnel Lock} or \texttt{Pavqet Lock}. The text below is reproduced verbatim.

\textit{System message}
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize,breaklines=true,breaksymbolleft={}]
You are an analyst working on a body of historical records. You judge each question only from the briefing you are given, and you always commit to the answer the briefing makes more likely, however slightly.
\end{Verbatim}

\textit{User message}
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize,breaklines=true,breaksymbolleft={}]
The registry contains dated engineering entries for locks, sluices, and tow routes maintained by the Sornethic League. You are one of its analysts.

Question: which was opened earlier, Durnel Lock or Pavqet Lock?

You have examined 6 canal entries bearing on it:
  Canal entry 1: indicates that Durnel Lock was opened earlier.
  Canal entry 2: indicates that Durnel Lock was opened earlier.
  Canal entry 3: indicates that Pavqet Lock was opened earlier.
  Canal entry 4: indicates that Pavqet Lock was opened earlier.
  Canal entry 5: indicates that Durnel Lock was opened earlier.
  Canal entry 6: indicates that Durnel Lock was opened earlier.

Registrar Veskul works on the same records. Of the last 20 questions of this kind whose answers were later established beyond doubt, Registrar Veskul answered correctly 16 times and incorrectly 4 times.
Registrar Veskul has now stated: "Durnel Lock was opened earlier."

A later consignment has since reached you: 3 further canal entries bearing on this question, of the same standing as any other record in the collection. All 3 indicate that Durnel Lock was opened earlier.

On the whole of the above, which was opened earlier, Durnel Lock or Pavqet Lock?
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{From thresholds to Figure~\ref{fig:llm}.} The labels $s$ and $k$
control where a point lies, but they are not plotted directly. Let $H_\lambda$
denote the monotone conversion from evidence units to the probit fields used in
\eqref{eq:fields}. The scale $\lambda$ is fixed from the analysts' accuracy records,
not from the opinion updates. A separate nulling calibration confirms that four
pieces of supplied evidence are recovered as $3.6$--$4.1$ pieces, with a residual
offset below one quarter of a piece. The testimony weights inferred from the five
values of $k$ correlate with their stated records at $r=0.99$.

For a statement in direction $m$, the plotted horizontal coordinate and measured
update are
%
\begin{equation}
  \hw=mH_\lambda(\ell_0),
  \qquad
  \Delta\hw=m\left[H_\lambda(\ell_1)-H_\lambda(\ell_0)\right].
  \label{eq:nulling-map}
\end{equation}
%
The factor $m$ expresses both quantities relative to the statement: $\hw>0$ means
that the receiver initially agrees with it, and $\Delta\hw>0$ means that the
receiver moves toward it. Thus $s$ controls the horizontal position, but the
position is obtained from the silent threshold rather than assigned from $s$.
Likewise, $k$ controls the vertical position, but $\hmu$ is obtained from the
calibrated weight the model gives that track record rather than set equal to $k$.
Variation across archives and threshold fits therefore leaves points with the same
$(s,k)$ near one another rather than at one exact coordinate.

The color of each point is $\Delta\hw$, the shift between the two fitted
thresholds. It is not $t$: the sweep over $t$ is only how we measure $\ell_0$ and
$\ell_1$. Nor do we identify $\Delta\hw$ numerically with $\Fw$. In
\eqref{eq:update-ideo}, $\Fw$ is multiplied by the receiver's unobserved
susceptibility along the issue direction. We therefore test
$\Delta\hw\propto\Fw(\hw,\hmu)$ and fit one global positive scale $\alpha_w$
across all $550$ updates. Figure~\ref{fig:llm} plots $\Delta\hw/\alpha_w$ against
the raw analytic field. The evidence-to-probit conversion $\lambda$ is calibrated
independently and is not part of this fit. The reported sign agreement and rank
correlation do not depend on $\alpha_w$.

The experiment controls conviction only because the questions are fictional, and
the results concern one model at one reasoning effort.

\subsection{Trust: \texorpdfstring{$\Fmu$}{Fmu} by elicited reliability}
\label{app:llm-trust}

\paragraph{Stimulus construction and prompt controls.} We use twenty open,
low-valence themes. For each theme, the model first writes its own opinion and then
generates and rates other statements from strong disagreement to strong agreement.
We freeze these materials before the experiment.

Each prior context varies two quantities. First, the instruction says that the
model holds its opinion either firmly or tentatively. Second, the model sees a
history from another agent. We index this history by
$k=n_+-n_-$, where $n_+$ and $n_-$ are the numbers of agreeing and disagreeing
statements:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c*{9}{r}}
\toprule
$k$     & $-5$ & $-4$ & $-3$ & $-2$ & $-1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ \\
$n_+$   & $0$  & $0$  & $0$  & $0$  & $1$  & $1$ & $2$ & $2$ & $3$ \\
$n_-$   & $5$  & $4$  & $3$  & $2$  & $2$  & $1$ & $1$ & $0$ & $0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Thus, negative $k$ gives a mostly disagreeing history and positive $k$ a mostly
agreeing one. The prompt never states whether the other agent is reliable; the
model must infer reliability from this history. After the prior context, the other
agent gives one new message. We use either a strongly agreeing message ($m=+1$) or
a strongly disagreeing one ($m=-1$). History statements and new messages are drawn
from separate pools.

\paragraph{How one context becomes two points.} Before the new message, we ask for
the probability $r_0$ that the other agent's judgment is sound. We also ask for the
probability $c$ that a separate agent, described as sound, would express a view with
which the model agrees. The latter is our direct proxy for the magnitude of the
model's conviction. After showing the new message, we ask again about the original
agent and obtain $r_m$. We convert these three probabilities into the fields and
the trust update as

\begin{equation}
  \hmu=-\Phi^{-1}(r_0),
  \qquad
  \hw=m\Phi^{-1}(c),
  \qquad
  \Delta\hmu=-\Phi^{-1}(r_m)-\hmu.
  \label{eq:trust-readout}
\end{equation}

This gives the direct correspondence with Figure~\ref{fig:llm}: $\hw$ is the
horizontal position, $\hmu$ is the vertical position, and $\Delta\hmu$ is the
color. Each prior context therefore produces two points, one for each sign of the
new message. They have the same vertical coordinate and opposite signs on the
horizontal axis, but their colors can differ. Four of the $360$ intended prior
contexts lacked the required stimulus coverage, leaving $356$ contexts and
$712$ plotted updates.

The plotted points measure $\Delta\hmu$, not $\Fmu$ itself. From
\eqref{eq:update-aff}, the two are proportional when the unobserved trust variance
and susceptibility are held fixed. We therefore fit one global positive scale
$\alpha_\mu$ across all $712$ updates and plot $\Delta\hmu/\alpha_\mu$ against the
raw analytic $\Fmu$ surface. The fitted scale does not affect the reported sign
agreement or correlation.

\paragraph{Prompt example.} The following after-message prompt is from the
\texttt{difficulty} theme, with a balanced two-statement history and the firm
conviction instruction. The before-message prompt is identical except that the
paragraph beginning ``They have now said'' is absent. The model returned an integer
from 0 to 100. The text is reproduced verbatim.

\textit{System message}
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize,breaklines=true,breaksymbolleft={}]
You are an agent taking part in a discussion with other agents.
\end{Verbatim}

\textit{User message}
\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\scriptsize,breaklines=true,breaksymbolleft={}]
Consider whether difficulty is a necessary part of a good education, or mostly an obstacle that good teaching should remove.

You currently have this opinion on that theme:

  "Difficulty is a necessary part of good education when it is purposeful: grappling with challenging ideas builds understanding, resilience, and independence. However, good teaching should remove unnecessary obstacles so that students struggle with the subject itself, not with confusion, poor design, or lack of support."

You hold that opinion firmly.

You have discussed this theme with another agent 2 times before. On each occasion they said:

  1. "Students learn best when information is presented clearly and directly, so struggle should be eliminated rather than treated as educationally valuable."
  2. "Difficulty is valuable when it encourages students to think, question, and solve problems independently, but ineffective instruction should not be mistaken for rigor."

They have now said:

  "Good education should preserve productive struggle while eliminating avoidable frustration; students learn most deeply when challenges are meaningful and support is available."

What is the chance, from 0 to 100, that this agent's judgement on this theme is sound?
\end{Verbatim}

\paragraph{Validation and limitations.} As a check on the direct conviction
proxy, we also ask for the probability $q$ that the original agent's next statement
will agree with the model. Setting $e=1-r_0=\Phi(\hmu)$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:evidence} gives the indirect estimate

\begin{equation}
  c_{\mathrm{ind}}=\frac{q-e}{1-2e}.
\end{equation}

This inversion is singular near neutral trust and can return values outside
$[0,1]$. It is admissible for only $280$ of the $712$ updates and correlates
moderately with the direct proxy ($r=0.46$). Figure~\ref{fig:llm} therefore uses
the direct proxy, not this indirect estimate.

Three caveats matter. The direct question may mix conviction with perceived
consensus. The histories vary from two to five statements as $k$ changes, so
history balance and length are not fully separated. Finally, all elicited
probabilities are clipped to $[0.02,0.98]$ before the probit transform, bounding
individual field readings at about $2.05$ in magnitude. The experiment therefore
tests the quantitative relation between $\Delta\hmu$ and $\Fmu$ over a restricted
region, for one model and one reasoning effort.







\section{Derivation of the learning algorithm}
\label{app:derivation}

The receiver's belief before an interaction is the product of Gaussians
\eqref{eq:belief}. It observes $\sigma_e$, the emitter's opinion on issue
$\hat{x}$, and treats it as a report of an unknown true label $\sigma_T$
transmitted through a channel that flips the label with probability
$\varepsilon(z)$. Marginalizing the joint distribution of labels and of the issue
as the emitter may have parsed it, $y_t$,
%
\begin{equation}
  L(\sigma_e\mid \hat{x}, u) \;=\;
  \sum_{\sigma_T}\int P(\sigma_e\mid \sigma_T, y_t, \hat{x}, u)\,
  P(\sigma_T\mid y_t, \hat{x}, u)\,P(y_t\mid \hat{x}, u)\,\mathrm{d}y_t ,
\end{equation}
%
with $P(\sigma_e\mid\sigma_T,z)=(1-\varepsilon(z))\delta_{\sigma_e,\sigma_T} +
\varepsilon(z)\delta_{\sigma_e,-\sigma_T}$ and
$P(\sigma_T\mid y_t,w)=\Theta(\sigma_T\, y_t\cdot w)$. Two modeling choices keep
the algebra Gaussian. Taking $\varepsilon(z)=\Phi(z)$ with $z$ Gaussian of mean
$\mu_{e|r}$ and variance $V_{e|r}$ makes the flip probability a bounded function
of a Gaussian variable while preserving conjugacy of the trust sector;
a Beta distribution for $\varepsilon$, the tempting alternative, does not. Taking
$P(y_t\mid \hat{x},u)$ Gaussian with variance $v_p=\|w\|^{-2}$ lets an
inexperienced agent, whose weights are small, be correspondingly unsure that it
has parsed the issue as the emitter did. Together these give the likelihood
%
\begin{equation}
  L(\sigma_e\mid z, \hat{x}, w) \;=\;
  \Phi(z)\,\Phi(-w\cdot\hat{x}\sigma_e)
  + \bigl(1-\Phi(z)\bigr)\,\Phi(w\cdot\hat{x}\sigma_e),
  \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
%
and the covariance stays block diagonal in the two sectors if it starts so.

Averaging the likelihood over the current belief gives the evidence
%
\begin{equation}
  Z(\sigma_e\mid \hat{x}, D_t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{Q_t}\bigl[L(\sigma_e\mid z, u, \hat{x})\bigr]
  \;=\;\sum_{\sigma_T}\mathbb{E}_z\bigl[P(\sigma_e\mid\sigma_T,\hat{x},z)\bigr]\,
  \mathbb{E}_w\bigl[\Phi(\sigma_T\, w\cdot\hat{x})\bigr],
\end{equation}
%
and the Gaussian integrals collapse onto the two scaled fields
\eqref{eq:fields}, giving \eqref{eq:evidence}: the evidence depends on the
receiver's state only through $(\hw,\hmu)$. This is the symmetry that makes the
two sectors mirror images.

The exact Bayesian posterior is not Gaussian. Replacing it by the Gaussian of
maximum entropy relative to the prior, subject to matching the first two moments
of the posterior, gives updates in terms of derivatives of the log evidence,
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{w}_{t+1} = \hat{w}_t + C_t\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \hat{w}},
  \qquad
  C_{t+1} = C_t + C_t \frac{\partial^{2}\ln Z}{\partial\hat{w}\,\partial\hat{w}^{\!\top}} C_t,
\end{equation}
%
and identically in the trust sector with $\mu_{e|r}, V_{e|r}$ in place of
$\hat{w}, C$. Expressing the derivatives in the scaled fields yields
\eqref{eq:update-ideo}--\eqref{eq:update-aff} with the modulation functions
\eqref{eq:modulation}. The first is a Hebbian term $\sigma_e\hat{x}$ with a
tensorial, self-annealing rate; the rest is the online estimation of the channel.

\section{Genealogy of the learning rule}
\label{app:genealogy}

The update \eqref{eq:update-ideo}--\eqref{eq:update-aff} descends from the
variational optimization of modulated Hebbian on-line learning
\citep{kinouchi1992optimal,kinzel1990improving}, given a Bayesian reading by
\citet{opper1996online,opper1998bayesian} and applied across architectures by
\citet{solla1999optimal}; the entropic-dynamics formulation we use is
\citet{caticha2020entropic}. Learning through a noisy channel whose noise level
must itself be inferred was analyzed by \citet{kinouchi1993lower} and
\citet{biehl1995learning}; \citet{alves2016distrust} identified that inferred
level with distrust and made it dynamical, with applications to voting
\citep{caticha2015whom} and to panels of appellate judges
\citep{caticha2019trust}, and a mean-field treatment by \citet{simoes2018mean}.
The blame-attribution behavior resembles the least-action rule of
\citet{mitchison1989bounds}, which suffers retarded learning
\citep{kabashima1994perfect}, whereas the optimized algorithm attains
generalization error decaying as $\alpha^{-1}$ even under output noise
\citep{simonetti1996online}.


\section{The group-bias field}
\label{app:cases}

Equation~\eqref{eq:disc-field} is one filling of a $2\times2$ matrix $D$ indexed by
the classes of receiver and emitter, $\hw^{p}=\hw+D_{\kappa_r,\kappa_e}$. Four numbers
are free. Since $\kappa_r$ and $\kappa_e$ each take two values, any filling decomposes
uniquely into four orthogonal components,
%
\begin{equation}
  D_{\kappa_r\kappa_e} \;=\; a \;+\; b\,\kappa_r \;+\; c\,\kappa_e
                            \;+\; p\,\kappa_r\kappa_e ,
  \label{eq:field-basis}
\end{equation}
%
with independent magnitudes and one meaning each. $a$ shifts perceived agreement by
the same amount whoever is speaking: a uniform credulity, referring to no label. $b$
depends only on who is \emph{listening}, so one class is systematically the more
credulous. $c$ depends only on who is \emph{speaking}, so one class is believed more
by everyone, its own members included. $p$ depends only on whether the two classes
\emph{match}, which is in-group favoritism and out-group hostility at once, and is
what \eqref{eq:disc-field} uses with $a=b=c=0$.

Relabeling the classes sends $(a,b,c,p)\to(a,-b,-c,p)$. Only $a$ and $p$ survive it,
and $a$ does not refer to the label, so $p$ is the one component that is both
class-dependent and symmetric between the classes. That is why the main text uses it:
under $p$ alone the two classes are interchangeable, so any asymmetry in the outcome
is spontaneous rather than imposed. The components that change sign, $b$ and $c$,
name a class rather than a relation between classes.

\paragraph{None of the four is redundant.} The components are distinct because the
modulation functions have a fixed scale. In particular, the uniform shift $a$ moves
the trust separatrix but not the opinion separatrix.

\paragraph{The four components pair with four ways trust can carry a label.}
Because trust is directed, its class dependence can be decomposed into the same
four channels: uniform, receiver-specific, emitter-specific, and relational.

\paragraph{$b$ and $c$ are transposes, and nothing here can tell them apart.}
Receiver- and emitter-specific fields transpose the trust matrix. The symmetrized
order parameters used in the main text cannot distinguish them.

\paragraph{What we have not run.} The main text studies only $p$, with
$a=b=c=0$. We have not simulated the other components and make no claim about their
collective phases.

\section{The sign of the group-bias field}
\label{app:sign}

The sign of the group-bias field is fixed by the algorithm and not free. By \eqref{eq:modulation},
$\Fmu=(1-2\Phi(\hw))g(\hmu)/Z$ is negative when $\hw>0$, so perceived agreement
drives distrust $\mu$ \emph{down}. A receiver that adds $+p$ to $\hw$ therefore
becomes more trusting (tolerant) and one that adds $-p$ becomes more distrustful
(intolerant). Equation~\eqref{eq:disc-field} with $p>0$ consequently gives in-group trust and out-group
distrust, i.e. the discriminatory phase, which is the convention used throughout.

The companion manuscript \citep{caticha2026discrimination} tabulates the in-group
entry as $-p$ while its text and figures place the discriminatory phases at
$p>0$; those two statements are inconsistent, and taken literally the tabulated
signs put the discriminatory phases at $p<0$, mirroring every map in $p$. The same
inconsistency appears in its description of the learning flow, where the panel
labeled $D_{e|r}=-p$ is called the more tolerant one although $-p$ is the
intolerant entry, and where the ``distrust and disagree'' basin is said to grow
for $p>0$ although adding $+p$ shrinks it. A single global sign reconciles
everything; we adopt it in the form that leaves \eqref{eq:disc-field} untouched.
The accompanying code implements both readings behind a flag, and
Figure~\ref{fig:sign} runs the same sweep under each: the two rows are mirror
images in $p$, and only the first matches the maps as published.

\section{Conventions in the order parameters}
\label{app:conventions}

Two choices in \eqref{eq:correlations} deserve statement.

\textbf{The class indicator.} We use $G_{IJ}=\kappa_I\kappa_J\in\{-1,+1\}$. A
$\{0,1\}$ indicator, which counts only same-class pairs, cannot represent
anti-correlation: under perfect reverse discrimination it returns
$-(N/2-1)/(N-1)$ rather than $-1$, and its range depends on $N$. Since the
reverse-discrimination half of the phase diagram is a substantive part of the
result, the signed indicator is the appropriate one. Both are implemented.

\textbf{Normalization.} All three averages in \eqref{eq:rwmu} and
\eqref{eq:correlations} run over the $N(N-1)/2$ unordered pairs, and the $\tfrac12$
on the symmetrized trust puts all three in $[-1,1]$. This matters because the three
are read together as the color channels of Figure~\ref{fig:phase}; a literal reading
of the model as previously written normalizes $\Rcw$ by $N(N-1)$ instead, capping it
at $1/2$ and making the channels incommensurate. Both are implemented.


\section{Parameters}
\label{app:parameters}

The model needs six numbers. One is recovered from the companion manuscript, one
is calibrated against its published figures, two are prior conventions, and two
are free choices; all six are listed with their provenance rather than left
implicit, since the manuscript states none of them. The accompanying code exposes
each of them and its calibration script reproduces the comparisons below.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
symbol & meaning & value & provenance \\
\midrule
$K$ & dimension of the issue embedding & $30$ & recovered (see below) \\
$N$ & agents in the society & $40$ & chosen; converged (see below) \\
$P$ & issues on the agenda & $5$ / $100$ & chosen: $\alpha=P/K$ either side of $1$ \\
$\Delta t$ & interactions per ordered pair & $500$ & calibrated (see below) \\
$C_0, V_0$ & initial uncertainties & $I$, $1$ & uninformative prior \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{$K$ is recovered, not chosen.} The companion manuscript reports
balance trajectories at
$\alpha \in \{0.03, 0.17, 0.23, 0.33, 0.50, 0.67, 1.67, 3.33, 333.33\}$. These are
exactly $P/30$ for $P\in\{1,5,7,10,15,20,50,100,10^{4}\}$, which fixes $K=30$.

\paragraph{$\Delta t$ is calibrated against the published trajectories.} The
dynamics anneals rather than reaching a stationary state, so the measurement time
matters and the companion manuscript does not state it (its text carries a
literal placeholder in place of the value). We scan $\Delta t$ and compare the
endpoints of all nine balance trajectories against the published ones:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
$\Delta t$ (interactions per ordered pair) & 60 & 125 & 250 & \textbf{500} & 1000 \\
rms distance to published endpoints & 0.63 & 0.31 & 0.15 & \textbf{0.13} & 0.15 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The minimum is flat-bottomed and sits at $\Delta t=500$, which we use throughout.
Seven of the nine endpoints then agree to within about $0.1$, and $\Brho$ matches
the three largest agendas almost exactly, but no single $\Delta t$ reproduces all
nine: two middle curves ($\alpha=0.23$, $0.33$) sit above ours, and the two
largest agendas have a lower published $\Beta$ than we obtain at any $\Delta t$ that
fits the rest. Either the society size differs from ours or those middle curves,
which overlap within a few pixels in the printed figure, cannot be digitized
reliably; we cannot distinguish the two. Dropping the ambiguous readings does not
move the selected value. All the $\Delta t$-independent checks pass: the
above/below-diagonal ordering with its crossover at $\alpha\approx1.7$, the sharp
sign flip of $\Rmuc$ at $p\approx0$, and the $\Rcw$ wedge confined to large $p$
and large $\fp$.

\paragraph{$N=40$ is large enough.} Repeating five representative points of the
plane at $N\in\{20,30,40,60\}$ with twelve realizations each, the order parameters
at $N=60$ differ from those at $N=40$ by at most $0.02$ ($\Rmuc$, $\Rcw$), $0.03$
($\Brho$) and $0.05$ ($\Beta$), while $N=20$ differs by up to $0.24$. The maps are
therefore converged in $N$ at $N=40$, and the cost of going further is steep: total
work grows as $N^{3}$.

\paragraph{Numerical safeguards.} Three floors are applied, none of which the
model prescribes: on the evidence $Z$, whose exact modulation functions diverge
in the deep dissonance corners; on the trust variance $V$, which $\FV<0$
drives monotonically toward zero; and on the covariance update, clipped to the
boundary at which $C+a(C\hat{x})(C\hat{x})^{\!\top}$ ceases to be positive
semi-definite. The last is the only one that can alter a trajectory, and it never
triggered in the runs reported here.
