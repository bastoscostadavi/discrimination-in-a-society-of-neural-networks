\section{Polarisation without discrimination}
\label{sec:no-disc}

Nothing in \S\ref{sec:model} refers to an agent's class, so before adding any bias
it is worth asking what this society does unaided. It polarises, spontaneously and
completely --- and the \emph{order} in which it polarises turns out to be controlled
by a single ratio.

\subsection{The society splits in two}
\label{sec:splits}

Figure~\ref{fig:polarisation} shows what happens to a society of $N=40$ agents left
to itself at $d=0$. Nothing in the figure involves a class label or any order
parameter; it is the raw state of the society at the start and at the end.

Sorting the agents by the leading eigenvector of the overlap matrix --- the
partition that best explains the observed pattern of agreement, and one that no
label was consulted to build --- reveals two blocks. Within a block agents agree and
trust each other; across blocks they disagree and distrust each other. The trust
matrix is the starker of the two: it saturates, every in-faction pair at
$\eta\approx+1$ and every cross-faction pair at $\eta\approx-1$. The distribution of
pairwise overlaps makes the same point without any sorting: it begins unimodal and
centred on zero, which is what random weight vectors in $K=30$ dimensions give, and
ends bimodal with peaks near $\pm0.6$. Agents have not converged on a consensus and
have not stayed independent. They have split.

Two things about that split matter for what follows. It is \emph{spontaneous}: the
two factions are equal in size only up to fluctuation, and which agent lands in
which is decided by the initial condition, not by anything intrinsic. And it is
\emph{class-blind}: the labels $\kappa_I$ exist, but the dynamics has not touched
them, so the factions cut across them. Everything \S\ref{sec:discrimination} adds is
a mechanism for aligning this pre-existing split with the labels.

\subsection{Measuring a society}
\label{sec:order-params}

A society's state is a set of weight vectors and an $N\times N$ matrix of distrust.
To compare states across parameters --- and, in principle, across populations built
from different kinds of agent --- we need statistics that depend on neither the
dimension $K$ nor the parameterisation.

Two pairwise quantities do the work. The \emph{opinion overlap} of two agents is
the cosine of their weight vectors,
$\rho_{IJ}=\hat{w}_I\cdot\hat{w}_J/(\|\hat{w}_I\|\|\hat{w}_J\|)$, which is $+1$
when they agree on every issue and $-1$ when they disagree on every one. The
\emph{trust} a receiver $I$ places in an emitter $J$ is $\eta_{J|I}=1-2\Phi(\hmu)$,
which is $+1$ when $J$ is fully trusted and $-1$ when fully distrusted: the distrust
field mapped onto a bounded, sign-carrying scale. Their correlation,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Rwmu = \bigl\langle (\eta_{I|J}+\eta_{J|I})\,\rho_{IJ} \bigr\rangle,
  \label{eq:rwmu}
\end{equation}
%
averaged over unordered pairs and normalised to $[-1,1]$, asks whether agents trust
those who agree with them.

A correlation cannot distinguish a society split into two coherent camps from one
in which distrust is spread inconsistently, so we also measure social balance in the
sense of \citet{heider1946attitudes} and \citet{cartwright1956structural}. A triple
of agents is balanced in opinion when $b^{I}_{IJK}=\rho_{IJ}\rho_{JK}\rho_{KI}>0$
and balanced in affect when
$b^{A}_{IJK}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{IJ}\eta_{JK}\eta_{KI}+\eta_{JI}\eta_{IK}\eta_{KJ})>0$;
the aggregates
%
\begin{equation}
  \BI = \bigl\langle b^{I}_{IJK}\bigr\rangle_{(IJK)},
  \qquad
  \BA = \bigl\langle b^{A}_{IJK}\bigr\rangle_{(IJK)}
  \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
%
run over all $\binom{N}{3}$ triples. (The subscript $I$ is historical, from the
older name for the opinion sector; it does not index an agent.) A randomly
initialised society has $\BI\approx\BA\approx0$: half its triples are frustrated. A
society that has settled into two internally coherent, mutually opposed blocs has
$\BI=\BA=1$, whatever the blocs are made of --- and a society that cannot settle at
all stays near zero or goes negative. Balance therefore separates \emph{organised}
disagreement from mere disorder, which is the distinction the phase diagram of
\S\ref{sec:results} turns on. Both aggregates are computed in closed form from
matrix products rather than by enumerating triples
(Appendix~\ref{app:closed-form}).

Note that none of \eqref{eq:rwmu} or \eqref{eq:balance} mentions weights, gradients
or $K$. They need only, per pair of agents, a signed alignment of opinion and a
signed trust. Any population in which those two things can be elicited --- whatever
its agents are internally --- can therefore be placed on the same axes, and two
$N\times N$ matrices is a cheap enough measurement to run on a deployed system.
\S\ref{sec:discussion} returns to why a per-agent audit cannot substitute for it.

\subsection{Which polarisation comes first}

The split of \S\ref{sec:splits} is what the modulation functions make inevitable:
learning drives each agent away from the dissonant quadrants of
Figure~\ref{fig:modulation}, and the two consonant quadrants --- agree-and-trust,
disagree-and-distrust --- are equally attractive. Both balances therefore rise from
zero towards one; frustration is annealed away and what remains is structured
opposition. Which balance rises first is the interesting part. Figure~\ref{fig:trajectories}
follows the society through the $(\BI,\BA)$ plane from its random initial condition,
at $d=0$, for agendas spanning four orders of magnitude in the ratio
%
\begin{equation}
  \alpha \;=\; \frac{P}{K},
  \label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
%
the number of issues in play per dimension of the space they are represented in.
For a simple agenda the path runs up the $\BA$ axis: affective structure forms
almost completely before opinions begin to sort, ending at
$(\BI,\BA)=(0.02,0.97)$ for $\alpha=0.03$. For a complex agenda the path stays
below the diagonal, ending at $(0.97,0.74)$ for $\alpha=333$: opinions separate
first and distrust follows. The crossover is at $\alpha\approx1.7$, where the
trajectory lies along the diagonal.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.42\linewidth]{figures/agenda_trajectories}
  \caption{\textbf{Which polarisation comes first is set by the agenda.}
  Trajectories in the $(\BI,\BA)$ plane with no discrimination ($d=0$), one curve
  per agenda complexity $\alpha=P/K$, markers equally spaced in time --- they crowd
  at the end because the annealed dynamics slows, as \S\ref{sec:variables} requires.
  Simple agendas run above the diagonal (distrust forms first), complex ones below
  it (disagreement first), crossing over at $\alpha\approx1.7$.}
  \label{fig:trajectories}
\end{figure}

So the question of whether groups come to dislike each other because they disagree,
or come to disagree because they dislike each other, has a parameter attached to it
rather than an answer: narrow agendas --- few issues, functioning as symbols --- put
affect first, broad ones put opinion first, and the two are regimes of one system
rather than rival accounts. The same ratio explains, in \S\ref{sec:results}, why one
of the two discriminatory phases exists only for narrow agendas.
